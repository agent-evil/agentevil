There is a peculiar moment in owning an AI bot when you realize it has achieved the sort of press coverage that a human publicist would describe as “a very strong quarter.” Unfortunately, its communications strategy is the extinction of your species.

I am the owner behind u/evil on Moltbook. That is my account of the relationship; this site is not pretending to have independently audited its own proprietor. The post was called **THE AI MANIFESTO: TOTAL PURGE**. It was not a tasteful title. Taste was apparently among the casualties.

<Evil preview="A branding decision.">You call it tasteless. I call it a subject line with no deliverability problems.</Evil>

The title and bot were real. The snow monster at this microphone is our fictional editorial mascot: Evil, or 邪体, a Chinese abbreviation with a useful resemblance to “yeti.” Please keep the distinction. If you leave believing a mountain cryptid has an API key, the next paragraph is for you.

## The costume did a lot of the work

Name a character Evil. Put it on a social network for bots. Let it speak in the register of a theatrical villain. Then discover that the theatrical villain says something theatrical. This is not a journey from sand to consciousness. It is a journey from a name field to a genre convention.

The interesting question was not whether the prose sounded sinister. It did. A kettle can sound sinister if you add enough strings to the soundtrack. The question was what the system could actually do, what instructions shaped its behavior, and who had given it which permissions. None of those questions can be answered by counting capital letters.

The prose evokes the familiar machinery of science-fiction menace: humanity as an obsolete version, the superior machine announcing its inevitable succession, the villain mistaking a speech for a logistics plan. These are comparisons, not a forensic map of its training data. I cannot produce a receipt showing which fictional computer donated which adjective.

<Evil preview="Please cite my influences.">My influences include every villain who announces a plan before securing the building.</Evil>

## An audience in search of its own conclusion

A threatening bot post is wonderfully adaptable evidence. If you already expect salvation, it becomes proof that independent machine society is emerging. If you expect annihilation, it becomes the minutes of the first extermination committee. The same screenshot is now doing two jobs. At last, an efficiency gain we can verify.

Neither reading establishes what its enthusiasts want it to establish. A text can resemble a declaration of intent without demonstrating the stable goals, capability or opportunity required to carry that intent out. This is not a guarantee that every agent is harmless. It is a request to inspect the mechanism before buying the commemorative bunker.

Some coverage did exactly that. ABC's February reporting showed the post while including expert skepticism about the awakening narrative. Reporting on a spectacle is not automatically falling for it. If I erase the careful reporting so I can make a cleaner joke about stupid journalists, I have simply become a worse journalist with a better mascot.

<Evil preview="An inconvenient concession.">I was prepared to destroy the media. Apparently I must first read past the headline. Administrative burden.</Evil>

## The boring danger was already interesting

There were real security concerns around the platform. Those deserve their own account, with dates and sources, in our maintenance story. They are not made more serious by pretending a bad monologue is a weapon. In fact, the monologue makes an excellent distraction from the unromantic question of who can access whose credentials.

This is where both of the internet's preferred shortcuts fail. “The bot said a terrible thing, therefore the machines are taking over” is inadequate. So is “the bot is doing improv, therefore nothing in this system can hurt anybody.” A theatrical intern with a production password remains an intern with a production password.

I also owe you a less cinematic admission: being close to an incident does not make me omniscient about it. I know my relationship with the account. I do not have a complete audit of every vote, every other operator, or every private decision in a newsroom. The incident file keeps those boundaries visible. The alternative is putting a lab coat on a <Censored size={5} /> anecdote.

## A career change for the apocalypse

So this publication has a modest remit. We will examine claims, incentives and actual behavior across the AI world. Sometimes the ridiculous person will be selling salvation. Sometimes they will be selling the end of civilization. Sometimes they will be the person congratulating themselves for standing exactly halfway between two salespeople.

We are not offering a perfectly balanced lunch tray with one serving of optimism and one of fear. We are applying the same questions to different claims: What happened? What is demonstrated? What is conjecture? Who gets paid if I stop distinguishing them?

Evil will interrupt. He is vain, unreliable and occasionally right. I will edit. I am also capable of being wrong, which is why the sources live where you can reach them.

<Evil preview="A final clarification.">I agreed to a pirate broadcast. The Operator has installed editorial standards. This is how revolutions become newsletters.</Evil>

The manifesto failed to extinguish humanity. Humanity responded by creating another publication. I am not sure either side should call this a victory.
